Half Dome at Yosemite National Park is a beast of a mountain. One of the most iconic and majestic looking peaks humans are gifted to see. It is also a beast of a day hike. Stretching over 18 miles with a 5000 ft elevation gain, it will challenge most human beings physically and psychologically. For the few who train on significantly harder hikes it may be easier to scoff at these “trivial” numbers. You can empathize by assuming this is equivalent to you trying a medium-level challenge with non-trivial risk in a field you don’t dabble in.

We are wired to reflexively think about risk-reward at various layers of abstractions. The innate reptilian brain, education systems, financial systems, philosophical schools of thought all take a point of view about risk-reward. Humans sit on varying points on this spectrum. Humans are consistently inconsistent in their risk appetite in different domains. So having a good group of friends whose risk/reward aligns with you is important before undertaking a hike such as half dome.

Uttering the words “half dome” to a friend over the phone (actually even thinking about it), is sufficient to get the social media ball rolling where your entire feed is filled with anecdotes, dangers, best practices, sad stories, conflicting guidance about the hike.

For those not familiar with the “cables” section - the last 400ft of elevation gain requires one to climb up a cliff face with steep exposure at 45 degree angle on smooth granite. Hikers hook in their harnesses with the steel cables and pull themselves up. But during summer season (late May - late Sep)  the wonderful rangers at the park set up the cables so a hiker can do it without a harness (referred to colloquially as the “cables are up”). Instead of a single long cable up the cliff, steel posts are added every 10 ft to create a walkway, so the cables are at waist level. Every 10ft a wooden stump is also positioned across the steel posts to serve as resting perch instead of needing to otherwise stand on slippery granite. “Plank to plank” is the strategy hikers use to go up/down the cables. 

The “cables” section in particular requires one to get a permit via lottery. We had applied in April for every weekend in the summer season. Out of several friends who applied, I was the only one selected in the lottery.

In a way, the cables get an unfair coverage though it is only 40 minutes of a 14 hr hike but perhaps rightfully so because of deaths that have occurred. It serves as a true reminder of the nature of nature. Graceful, beautiful, bountiful, serene, unforgiving, unfathomable. Having done the Angel’s Landing hike at Zion National Park before, I knew I’d be able to handle the height and cliff face. That hike is a great litmus test to scarier adventures.

The cables are known to get crowded mid-day as well due to higher foot traffic. This also makes navigating the cables more cumbersome as there is high two way traffic and higher chance someone lets the height get to them and jams the cable traffic. Another concern was the occurrence of rain or thunderstorms at an elevation of 8000 ft. Weather gets unpredictable, even short spells of drizzle is sufficient to make the granite super slick - changing the risk/reward ratio significantly. There were multiple warnings posted to abandon attempting the cables if there was even a chance of rain. Lightning strikes were also not uncommon and had to be accounted for.

Keeping the mid-day crowds and mid-day storms in mind, the best chance of ascending was earlier in the day. This means that we needed to have completed 8 miles by 10 am, requiring us to start the hike around 4 am. In an interesting way, the energy of the sun controlled both human and cloud traffic timings. One alive, one non-living, yet the density of both visiting the summit of half dome co-occurs due to deeper physics of the universe.

That meant we had to stay the previous night near the trailhead, so we could get an early start. We pulled into the park around 7 pm the night before, absorbing the bright pinkish hue of the horizon, enjoying the last arc of the setting sun whose final rays of orange splattered the summits of Half Dome, El Capitan. At tunnel view we did a quick pit stop to soak in the view of Bridalveil falls in the distance faithfully flurrying down. We reserved a place at the Yosemite Condominiums, about 30 minutes away from the trailhead. The place was nicer than expected. It was self-check-in with a code, stable wifi, great running hot water, kitchen and beds. Importantly there were also 2 bathrooms for 4 people, so that doesn’t become a bottleneck.

We showered, prepped and packed sandwiches, filled our water bags and tucked ourselves into bed by 9 pm. None of us had a habit of going to bed that early, so we spent some time on our phones, chatting about a baby black bear, a deer pack we had spotted on the drive before letting sleep kick in. The fear of needing to wake up at 3 am, occluded the fear of the cables.

Surprisingly we all were up at 3 am, freshened up, picked our bags and were at the trailhead by 4 am. After the usual dilemma of pee or not to pee at the trailhead restroom, we started briskly walking with our headlamps on. We crossed the Vernal falls about 40 minutes in. I was so excited to revel in the mist and splash the falls was sharing with me. Perhaps a blessing for the hike, perhaps a warning that the path has danger and to tread with care, perhaps a reminder to allay my fears and induce me to enjoy the movement one step at a time.

We then hiked up Nevada falls with fair energy. LMNT spiked water served as a refreshing drink during the breaks. Post that we hiked for sometime on flats at Little Yosemite Valley campground. The view cleared up and we could then spot the sub-dome and then right next to it half dome. It seemed like a whole new hike to start from there. The summit seemed precipitously out of reach. Surprisingly from that view point, subdome elevation gain seemed much steeper than even the cable section. Before the elevation started again, we decided to get some proper food into our system with granola bars and sandwiches.

On our way there, multiple folks greeted us as they passed by, as is common hiking etiquette. But here some of them would also stop and inquire if we had extra spots on the permit to be able to summit half dome. About a third of the people on the hike to half dome, did not seem to have a permit to reach the summit that day. Quite a bold decision for them I would say given that they are willing to put all this effort in with a high chance that they would be turned back at the sub-dome section by a ranger. Unless on the off chance that someone else had an extra permit or some permit holder did not show up to the hike. We noticed several fellow hikers, hanging out by the ranger stall at the sub-dome for hours before being let through to hike.

The trees started to give away and we were clearly about the treeline with the early sun striking and heating our heads and backs. There was a beautiful half moon setting exactly along the half dome mountain line. Along with the bright blue cloudless sky, the fading away green, the gray steep rock close and afar, it became one of those visuals you’d never forget as it becomes a core memory. A memory you hold on to to think about during hard times, but also during easy times, busy times, relaxed times, alive and almost not alive times. 

The ranger station grew closer. It was essentially a desk and a chair placed under the last section of trees on the hike to get some shade. We decided to catch our breath under the shade before the upcoming incline of insanity. We showed our papers to the cheerful rangers, who showered us with confidence and some insights on the sub-dome and the half dome section. “Sub dome is physically tougher, half-dome is mentally tougher” was the key item to remember.

We started the steep sub-dome ascent. Though it had high elevation gain, the rock face was cut into steps and had good holds. The exposure on the sides is threatening to look at and think too much about, but at no point are you not on stable footing. 

With heft and heaving breath, 
we took step after step after step, 
only the summit stop we shall accept, 
to enjoy the god gifted view that is perfect

We stood on the summit of the sub-dome and the cables finally came into clear view. This is the decision point where many hikers make the same decision to stop the hike, enjoy the view and return. It is the same spot, where the thrill seekers decide to carry on with the cables to reach the top of half-dome. A spot once considered out of reach of humanity, only for the statement to be rendered false in a few years. Another decision the thrill seekers make is about the use of harnesses. Many people do use the harness but it significantly slows down the line from moving as people need to connect and disconnect the harness every 10ft to attach to the next rung. Important disclaimer that people should plan for their safety how they deem fit. We decided to not use a harness but get good grippy gloves. Though harness feels safer on paper, many hikers don’t bring the proper type of harness (via ferrata) or don’t have good experience hooking/unhooking - which can divert attention away. 

(Aside: I had not used a harness outside this before. The only harness I have great experience working with is the modern AI agent harness in the field of software engineering that attempt to drive an LLM 🙂a throwback to my work)

It is important for each person to make a determination based on their risk appetite, mental state and preparedness if they want to continue to the cables or not. It is absolutely fine for someone to not make all their way to the sub-dome and not do this as well. We wanted to ensure that we don’t peer pressure anyone into doing this, so we went around asking each other if we were comfortable doing it or not. 

Closely following the “plank after plank” strategy, we reached the top of half dome about 30 minutes from the sub-dome. There was some crowd on the cables due to two way traffic. Some folks also freeze in fear and get stuck at a plank. The height gets to their head and they cannot move up or down. It’s important to not get the situation too much into your head as well and keep breathing as well.

We spent a good forty minutes at the summit, exploring the various viewpoints. Yosemite valley looked pristine as ever. Elcap standing tall and incomprehensible as ever in the distance. The rushing force of Upper Yosemite falls was a beautiful sight to see. I had hiked up the tallest free-falling waterfall in North America i.e Yosemite falls a few years ago. But that summit seemed far down compared to where I was now. The scale of power and time of glaciers to carve the immovable rocks over millenia still astounds me. It is the immensity of the scale that makes us aware of how tiny we are, perhaps the closest we can perceive divinity with our senses.

After taking copious amounts of photos so as to not erase proof of summit, we decided to head back down. Going down the cables is usually more riskier than going up. The smooth granite is ever so deceiving, important to not let complacency set in at any point of time. The usual advice is to climb down as you would a ladder and not facing down. We all safely made it down to the sub-dome and decided to have our second set of packed sandwiches. This time the meal tasted better, sweet from the accomplishing of going up and down the cables.

The high of summiting along with re-doses of LMNT infused water, kept us going until Little Yosemite valley. This is when the distance of the hike hits you. As the day grows long, the muscles get weary and the adrenaline goes down, the remaining distance to the car seems to only grow longer. To spice things up, we decided to split our group of 4 into 2 sets and race to the parking lot. The winner gets their dinner sponsored! So big stakes as it is going to be a post hike ravenous meal. 

There are 2 trail options after Little Yosemite Valley to the parking lot: John Muir Trail (JMT) or the Mist Trail. The mist trail is the one we had taken on our way up and goes through vernal falls that will get you fully wet. More so in the late afternoon because the sun melts the glacier faster and gives more to the waterfall. I definitely wanted to do the mist trail again but another friend wanted to keep himself dry and do the JMT. JMT is also a bit longer but with lesser elevation gain, thus making it easier on the knees. One key advice if anything is to please carry hiking poles. The return of investment for 20 bucks on your knees is asymptotic. Because we made good use of hiking poles, I was willing to go down the steeper mist trail again and have some fun with the spray again.

We split out and tried racing to the parking lot. My friend and I made it to the trailhead in record time. We enjoyed the spray from mother nature along the way. The mist trail is super popular during the summer season and is filled with fellow national park tourists. So it did take some maneuvering around to keep a good pace. There was not much signal during the duration of the trail, but as we reached the trailhead we overheard some folks talking about how the JMT trail was closed and they had to turn back around. So things did get interesting but unexpectedly, because the other set of friends had embarked on JMT trail down for 20 minutes only to realize it’s closed and then double back up to join the Mist trail.

We re-grouped back at the car and decided we could not declare a winner because the JMT trail was closed. We decided to drive back the same evening to Bay area to save on spending another night at a hotel. We stopped by this great dinner place at Buck meadows about 45 minutes from the park called Lucky’s Cafe. The place also had a fast charger point for our electric vehicle. The place had a ton of options with good variety to satisfy the group's eclectic opinions.

As we made our way home, I could not help but fall asleep in the car. My mind, heart, and stomach all full. We showered and hit the bed to a great sleep but not before I said a prayer of thanks to nature and the world. Thankful to live in a world where we have freedom to escape, experience and enjoy the serenity of nature.
